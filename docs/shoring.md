# Shoring: trench boxes, slide rail, hydraulic shores, sheet piles, soldier piles

An excavation can be shored instead of sloped. Pick the support in its P panel and it's drawn in
the pit to scale, with a count of what goes in, the wall area it holds and a rough weight, so you
can see what the job needs on site and plan where it's staged. The library's **Shoring** category
has the same pieces to lay down before they go in.

**This is a planning aid, not an engineered design.** The sizes, counts and weights are typical
figures for laying out a site and talking to suppliers. Real shoring is chosen from the
manufacturer's tabulated data for the soil, depth and loads (surcharge from spoil, cranes and
traffic, water), or designed by a professional engineer, and checked on site by a qualified
person.

## Using it

1. Draw an excavation (library **L** → *Tools* → **Excavation**) or select one, and press **P**.
2. Set **support**: *none / sloped* (the default), *Trench box*, *Slide rail*, *Hydraulic shores*,
   *Sheet piles* or *Soldier piles + lagging*.
3. With any support the sides are cut vertical: the pit is redug with straight sides, and
   **side slope** is greyed out. Set support back to *none / sloped* and the slopes come back.

The shoring is rebuilt from the pit's **width**, **length** and **depth** whenever they change, and
follows the pit when it's moved or turned. Unlike the pit's orange outline it shows all the time,
library open or closed: it's a real thing on site. It's saved with the layout.

Supports that line only two sides (trench boxes and hydraulic shores) run along the longer side
of the rectangle, whichever way it was drawn.

## The options

### Trench box

Two steel side panels held apart by spreader pipes, dropped into a trench and dragged along as the
pipe goes in. For utility trenches, where people work between the panels.

- Drawn: safety-yellow panels 120 mm thick with ribs down the outside, a channel along the top and
  collar posts where the 150 mm (6 in) spreaders pin on: two per end (near the top and the bottom)
  in a box, one per end in a stacker. Lifting eyes on top.
- Sizes as they're sold: panels 2.4 m (8 ft) tall, and 1.2 m (4 ft) stackers; lengths 2.4, 3.0,
  3.7, 4.9, 6.1 and 7.3 m (8, 10, 12, 16, 20 and 24 ft).
- Along the trench: of the runs that leave less than the shortest box (2.4 m) uncovered, the one
  with the fewest boxes, then the longest run, longest boxes first, centred. A box may be up to 5 cm
  longer than the trench. For example 6 m takes one 16 ft box, 13.4 m a 24 and a 20, 20 m two 24s
  and a 16, 25.5 m three 24s and a 10.
- Up: 2.4 m boxes, with a 1.2 m stacker on top where that's enough, until the top is level with
  the rim or above it. 1.5 and 2.4 m deep take one box, 3 m a box and a stacker, 5 m two boxes
  and a stacker. A pit no deeper than 1.2 m gets a 1.2 m box.
- The panels stand against the long sides. Over about 4.5 m between the panels the spreaders don't
  reach: it's still drawn, with a warning to use slide rail or sheet piles.
- The ends are open, as on real boxes: the readout says they need end plates or sloping, and how
  much of each end the boxes don't reach.

Readout, e.g. a 3 x 20 m trench 3 m deep: *6 boxes: 2 × 2.4 × 7.3 m (8 × 24 ft) + 1 × 2.4 × 4.9 m
(8 × 16 ft) + 2 × 1.2 × 7.3 m (4 × 24 ft) + 1 × 1.2 × 4.9 m (4 × 16 ft), 2 high, 2.8 m between
panels; top 0.7 m above the rim*, 117 m² of shored wall, about 17 t of steel.

### Slide rail

Steel posts set at the corners and along the sides, with panels sliding down between them as the
pit is dug. For manholes, pump stations and pits where a box won't do, with all four sides held.

- Drawn: yellow posts at the corners and every 4 m or less, grey panels between them on all four
  sides, stacked like trench boxes (2.4 m, with 1.2 m on top) to the rim or above. The posts stand
  at least 0.6 m out of the ground.
- In a trench up to 6 m wide, a strut across between each pair of facing posts.
- Most systems go to about 7.5 m deep; deeper gets a warning.

### Hydraulic shores

Pairs of aluminium rails pressed against the trench walls by hydraulic cylinders across the trench.
Light and quick to set from the top, for trenches in reasonably firm ground.

- Drawn: a shore every 2.4 m (8 ft) or less along the trench, starting 0.6 m in from the ends; the
  top cylinder 0.45 m below the rim and the rest spread down to 0.75 m above the floor, no more
  than 1.2 m apart (one row up to 1.65 m deep, 2 rows at 2.4 m, 3 at 3 m). The trench walls
  between the rails are drawn as earth faces.
- Cylinders reach about 4 m: wider gets a warning. Tabulated data generally stops at 6 m (20 ft)
  deep: deeper gets a warning too.
- The ends are open, and in loose or wet ground the rails need plywood sheeting behind them: the
  readout says so.

### Sheet piles

Interlocking steel sheets driven round the pit before it's dug, holding back soil and much of the
water. For deep pits, soft ground and anywhere near the water, as on the berths here.

- Drawn: Z-profile sheets about 0.6 m wide and 0.38 m deep, all round the outline, standing 1 m out
  of the ground, with a steel waler (a W360 on its side) round the inside 0.7 m down.
- Deeper than 4 m: struts across the short way (up to 12 m across) every 4.5 m or less, and braces
  across the corners of pits 6 m and wider. Wider than 12 m it says to use rakers or tie-backs.
- Sheet length: 1 m out of the ground, the depth, and a toe below the floor of about the depth
  again for a cantilever wall (4 m deep or less) or 0.6 of it when braced, at least 1.5 m, rounded
  up to 0.5 m. A rough planning length: the real one comes from the design.

Readout, e.g. a 12 x 12 m pit 5 m deep: *80 sheets, 48 m of wall, 9.0 m long each (Z-profile, 0.6 m
wide); waler round the inside, 2 struts across, 4 corner braces*, 240 m², about 55 t of steel.

### Soldier piles + lagging

Steel H-piles driven or drilled in round the pit, with timber boards (lagging) slotted in behind
their flanges as it's dug. Cheaper than sheet piles where water isn't a problem; not watertight.

- Drawn: HP310 piles about 2.4 m apart round the outline (one at each corner), standing 0.5 m out
  of the ground, with 75 x 300 mm lagging boards down to the floor.
- Pile length: 0.5 m out, the depth, and a toe of 0.75 of the depth (at least 1.5 m).
- Deeper than 4 m it says they need walers with struts, rakers or tie-backs.

## P panel readouts

| Readout | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| shoring | What goes in and how many: boxes and their sizes, sheets and their length, piles and lagging |
| shored wall | Area of excavation wall the support holds, m² |
| weight (approx.) | Rough weight of the steel (aluminium for hydraulic shores), from typical weights: steel box panels 105 kg/m², slide rail panels 120 kg/m² and posts 200 kg/m, Z-sheets 65 kg/m, HP310 piles 79 kg/m |
| shoring notes | Warnings: open ends, too wide or too deep for the system, needs bracing or an engineer |

The shoring lines are left out for an unsupported pit. The **floor** readout is the full width and
length for a shored pit, as its sides are vertical.

## WorkSafeBC

In British Columbia, excavations are covered by Part 20 of WorkSafeBC's Occupational Health and
Safety Regulation. Among other things, nobody goes into an excavation over 1.2 m (4 ft) deep until
its sides are sloped, benched or supported, and the support for one over 6 m (20 ft) deep is
designed by a professional engineer. So the notes say:

- An unsupported excavation deeper than 1.2 m with sides steeper than 1 : 1 (including
  *vertical*): *Over 1.2 m deep: needs sloping, benching or shoring before anyone goes in
  (WorkSafeBC Part 20).* A 1 : 1 slope is a common starting point, but the ground may need flatter.
- Any excavation deeper than 6 m: the sides or their support must be designed by an engineer.

These are reminders for planning, not a check against the regulation: read the current
regulation and its guidelines, and have a qualified person look at the actual ground.

## How it's drawn

- The ground is a grid of heights 0.5 m apart, so a vertical side is a steep face about 1 m wide
  centred on the outline (see [earthworks.md](earthworks.md)). The shoring stands in that face at
  the outline, so a little soil shows at the foot of the walls inside, and behind walls that hold
  soil (all but trench boxes) a strip of earth at rim level covers the gap. A trench box stands
  in a trench dug a little wider than itself, as on site.
- The volumes are the pit's: W x L x depth for a shored pit, whatever the shoring.
- Shoring isn't solid: you walk and drive through it, and clicking it selects the pit.
- Repeated parts are merged into one mesh per material, so a 60 x 40 m sheet-piled pit is four
  draw calls.

## For developers

`src/shoring.js` plans and draws the support from the excavation's params; `src/shoring-models.js`
builds the pieces, for both the pits and the library. `window.twin.shoring.planShoring( params )`
gives the plan (summary, wall area, weight, notes) for any `{ width, length, depth, slope, support }`,
and `boxRun( length )` / `boxStack( depth )` in `shoring.js` the trench box choice (in feet).
`npm run test:shoring-ui` checks it all in a browser without Google's tiles.
